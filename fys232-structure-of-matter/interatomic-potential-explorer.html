What to look for

Two anchor numbers. Both models are built from the same two inputs: the minimum sits at the bond length r₀, where the force F = −dU/dr vanishes, and it lies a depth D below the energy of the separated atoms, U(∞) = 0. D is therefore the energy needed to pull the bond apart completely (the dissociation energy, measured from the bottom of the well). The models differ in how they connect these two points.

Repulsive wall. When the electron clouds start to overlap, the Pauli exclusion principle pushes the atoms apart. This is the same physics as the A/rⁿ term in the ionic-solid model of Sect. 2.4. LJ uses r⁻¹²; Morse uses an exponential and stays finite at r → 0.

Attractive tail. LJ's −r⁻⁶ term is the van der Waals attraction between neutral atoms: shallow and long-ranged (try Ar₂). Morse falls off exponentially and is the usual fit for covalent bonds such as H₂ and N₂, where the attraction comes from the shared electron pair (Sect. 2.2). Sect. 2.2 builds the H₂⁺ curve from two competing terms, the electron energy that falls as the protons approach and the proton–proton repulsion that rises, and the sum has a minimum near 0.11 nm; use the H₂⁺ preset.

Force and vibration. The steepest part of the attractive branch is the inflection point of U; there |F| is largest (Fmax = aD/2 for Morse, ≈ 2.69 D/r₀ for LJ) and it is the force needed to pull the bond apart. Because F = −dU/dr, the shaded area under the attractive branch is exactly D. Close to r₀ the force is a spring, F ≈ −k(r − r₀) with k = U″(r₀) (switch on the harmonic approximation), which gives the vibration ħω = ħ√(k/μ) of Sect. 2.3 and the zero-point correction D₀ ≈ D − ħω/2. Matching the two models at the minimum requires a = 6/r₀; real covalent bonds have a much smaller a, so LJ is far too stiff for them.

Preset values. H₂: r₀ = 0.0741 nm, D = 4.75 eV, ωe = 4401 cm⁻¹. H₂⁺: 0.106 nm, 2.79 eV, 2321 cm⁻¹. N₂: 0.1098 nm, 9.91 eV, 2359 cm⁻¹. In each case the Morse a follows from ωe = (a/2πc)√(2D/μ). Ar₂ uses typical LJ parameters of argon (ε = 10.3 meV, σ = 0.3405 nm, so r₀ = 21/6σ = 0.382 nm); the real dimer has re ≈ 0.376 nm and D ≈ 12.3 meV.
