How the model works, and what it can and cannot predict

Heat capacity

Only the dispersion enters. For each of the three acoustic branches (one longitudinal, two transverse; N modes each, common cut-off kD = (6π²n)1/3):

C = Σbranches ∫₀kD (k²/2π²) kB x²eˣ/(eˣ−1)² dk,   x = ħω(k)/kBT

For ω = v k this is exactly the Debye result of Sec. 4.2. In the empty-lattice picture the same linear ω = v|k| is only folded back into the first Brillouin zone, which moves the branches around but leaves the density of states unchanged, so the folded and the extended picture give the same C(T). For metals the free-electron (empty-lattice) gas adds Cel = (π²/2) z R (T/TF) (m*/m), with TF from the valence-electron density.

Thermal conductivity

Kinetic theory of the phonon gas, Eq. (4.3.52), done mode by mode instead of with one average:

κ = ⅓ Σ ∫ (k²/2π²) ck vg² τk dk,   1/τ = vg/L + A ω⁴ + B ω² T e−θ/3T

The three terms are boundary (Casimir), isotope/point-defect (Rayleigh, with Tamura’s mass-variance parameter Γ) and Umklapp scattering. The dispersion by itself gives ck and vg but not the mean free path. The strength of the Umklapp term is controlled by one number, γeff, in B = ħγeff²/(M v² θ). Metals also get κe = L₀T/ρ(T) (Wiedemann–Franz, ρ from Bloch–Grüneisen).

Einstein solid (test material)

Every atom sits in its own cage with the same frequency ωE, so the heat capacity is the Einstein function and, without coupling, nothing propagates (vg = 0, κ = 0). Nearest-neighbour coupling of relative strength c turns the flat level into a band, ω² = ωE²(1 + 4c sin²(πk/2kD)). The same scattering terms are used, with the band top θU = ħωmax/kB and v = ωmax/kD taking the place of the Debye θ and v in the Umklapp and isotope rates. The model has a gap and no acoustic branch, so the low-temperature side of the κ peak is thermally activated (∝ e−θE/T) instead of ∝ T³. It is a toy model for studying how bandwidth and group velocity control the position and height of the peak, not a description of a real crystal.

What comes out

  • C(T): predicted from the dispersion alone: T³ law, T/θ scaling, Dulong–Petit limit, γT for metals. Known weak point: the linear (acoustic-only) model gives C(300 K) ≈ 3.8 J/mol·K for diamond, the measured value is 6.1, because the real transverse branches flatten and optical phonons are already populated. The sine dispersion repairs most of this (≈ 6.6).
  • κ(T) shape: reproduced without further input: rise ∝ T³ (boundary-limited), a maximum, then a fall ∝ 1/T at high T, and a peak position set by θ. Diamond’s peak lies near 100 K, silicon’s near 25–30 K.
  • κ(300 K) of diamond: the ranking is predicted (a factor of ~25 above silicon) because θ is high (many fewer phonons excited, C small but v large, and Umklapp freezes out as e−θ/3T). The absolute value needs one calibrated γeff. With γeff = 2.2 fixed on silicon at 300 K the model gives roughly germanium 58 (exp. 60), silicon at 100 K about 910 (exp. ≈ 880), and diamond about 4000, i.e. the right order but ~1.7× too high.
  • Not captured: normal (momentum-conserving) phonon processes, optical branches, real anisotropic dispersion, and the full size of the isotope effect (the model gives about +9 % at 300 K for pure ¹²C diamond; measured +44 %, Table 4.2). Peak heights at low T depend strongly on L and on defects; the model overestimates the 41 000 W/m·K of ¹²C-enriched diamond near 104 K.
