assumptions & caveats

This is an order-of-magnitude instrument, not a simulation. Peak intensity assumes a Gaussian temporal profile (I ∝ exp(−4ln2·t²/τ²), so τ is FWHM) and a simple circular focal area from the stated spot diameter; real focusing (M², aberrations) typically costs a factor of a few. The field is the peak of a linearly-polarized plane wave, E₀ = √(2I/cε₀). a₀ = 0.85·λ[µm]·√(I/10¹⁸ W cm⁻²) is the standard normalized vector potential for linear polarization. T_hot uses the Wilks ponderomotive scaling. The Keldysh parameter γ = ω√(2mI_p)/(eE₀) uses hydrogen's ionization potential (13.6 eV) as a generic reference atom. Regime boundaries in the plot are illustrative bands, not sharp thresholds.

Technology tiers (used by the target-intensity slider to pick a laser class) are representative, illustrative ceilings, not hard physical limits: an oscillator up to ~3 W average power at 1–100 MHz; a kHz-class CPA amplifier up to ~20 W average power at 1 Hz–1 MHz; a PW-class facility up to ~300 W average power (or 500 J per pulse, whichever binds) at 0.01–1 Hz. Given a target intensity, pulse duration and spot size, the app solves for the pulse energy needed, picks the least exotic tier that can supply it, and sets the repetition rate to whatever that tier's average-power budget allows — exactly as a real lab has to trade rep rate for pulse energy at fixed cooling/pump power. Pulse energy is always E = P_avg / f_rep. The Schwinger limit (2.3×10²⁹ W cm⁻²) is shown for scale — no laser comes close. Direct, field-driven neutron ejection via nuclear polarizability would need ~10³⁹ W cm⁻², off the top of this entire console, and is not achievable by any conceivable laser; real laser-driven neutron sources work indirectly through the bremsstrahlung route described below.
