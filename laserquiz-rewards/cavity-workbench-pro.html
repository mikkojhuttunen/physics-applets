Laser cavity workbench

Place mirrors, crystals, etalons and filters in order around the beam path. Beam size comes from ABCD matrices (tangential and sagittal); threshold, mode selection and linewidth follow from the round-trip loss and the spectral filters.

Workbench

Cavity type

Elements are listed in the order the beam meets them. Drag a card (or use ◀ ▶) to reorder. The gap is the free-space distance to the next element; in a ring the last gap returns to element 1; in a linear cavity the first and last elements are the end mirrors and the beam passes the middle elements twice.

Both rays start just before element 1 and use the same round-trip ABCD matrices as the beam calculation. In a stable cavity they oscillate about the axis forever; in an unstable one they walk off and are lost at the aperture (✕). Change a gap or a mirror and watch how quickly the rays escape: that tells you which way to move towards the stable region.

In a standing wave the lasing mode saturates the gain only at its antinodes; at the nodes, every λ/2n, the gain stays near its small-signal value. Mode q + m has its antinodes shifted by φ = 2π m p / prt, where p is the optical distance from the end mirror and prt the round-trip path, so it can feed on that left-over gain. In a ring with an optical isolator the wave travels, the intensity is uniform and no gain is left over. Things to try: move the crystal right next to an end mirror, add a twisted-mode pair, or switch to a ring with an isolator.

Pump and environment

Tilting a curved mirror by θ makes it astigmatic: fT = (R/2)cos θ, fS = R/(2cos θ). A Brewster-cut crystal is astigmatic the opposite way (effective lengths L/n³ and L/n). With the right fold angle the two cancel and the mode in the crystal is round.

Target

Model assumptions – read before trusting a number
  • Optics. Paraxial ABCD matrices in reduced distances (d/n), so the q-parameter eigenvalue of the round trip directly gives the physical beam radius. Curved mirrors at incidence angle θ: fT = (R/2)cos θ, fS = R/(2cos θ). Brewster-cut slabs: effective distances L/n³ (tangential) and L/n (sagittal), with L the path length inside the crystal (Kogelnik).
  • Ray trace. Paraxial rays (y, n·θ) pushed through the same round-trip matrices, so they exist whether or not a Gaussian eigenmode does. The round-trip Gouy phase θ = arccos m sets how fast a ray circles the axis; if θ/2π is close to a fraction p/q with small q the ray closes on itself after q round trips and transverse modes become degenerate. For |m| > 1 a ray grows by |m| + √(m² − 1) per round trip. Apertures only stop rays; their diffraction loss is not modelled.
  • Astigmatism compensation. The button scans one common incidence angle θ for all curved mirrors and keeps the one where both planes see the same cavity, mT = mS (equal stability ranges, round beam in the arms). For comparison it quotes the textbook two-mirror condition for the Brewster crystal, R sin θ tan θ = L(n²−1)/n³ with L the path length in the crystal (Kogelnik et al. 1972). Brewster surfaces elsewhere (BRF, plates) also enter the scan.
  • Layout. A four-mirror ring is drawn as a bowtie from the real arm lengths, with the crystal arm at the bottom and the two arms horizontal; only the horizontal direction is stretched to fill the panel. The fold angles that close this bowtie are compared with the incidence angles you entered, which are what the beam calculation uses.
  • Spatial hole burning panel. One mode q lases alone; its gain is clamped so that its own modal gain equals the loss. Along the crystal the saturated gain is g(z) = g₀/(1 + S·I(z)), with the standing-wave intensity I = sin²(k p) in a linear cavity and I = 1 in a ring with an isolator; S follows from the clamping condition. Mode q + m sees the overlap of g(z) with its own pattern, which is phase-shifted by 2π m p/prt, times its own small-signal gain and loss (gain line and filters). A bar above 1 means that mode can start. Pump absorption along the crystal, transverse profiles and the second mode's own saturation are ignored. In a ring without an isolator the standing-wave position is not pinned by a mirror; the panel assumes equal counter-propagating beams whose patterns coincide at the crystal centre. A twisted-mode pair is treated as making the intensity in the crystal uniform. The mode count in the results uses the simpler rule under Mode selection, so the two can differ.
  • Thermal lens. Thin lens at the crystal centre, f = πKwp²/(Pheat·dn/dT) with Pheat = quantum defect × absorbed pump. It ignores end-face bulging, stress birefringence and aberrations, and overestimates the lens for very tight pump spots, so use the calibration factor if you have a measured value. The Ti:sapphire example uses 0.3 as an assumption, not a measurement.
  • Threshold and power. Ideal four-level rate-equation estimate: Pth = hνpπ(wl²+wp²)γ / (2·Npass·σ·τ), slope = (λp/λl)·ηoverlap·0.9·T/γ. No excited-state absorption, upconversion or reabsorption, so thresholds are optimistic.
  • Mode selection. Every cavity mode inside the gain window is given a ratio x = (gain)/(round-trip loss incl. filter losses). The best mode wins. In a travelling-wave ring (ring + optical isolator) homogeneous broadening allows only that one mode; the margin to the runner-up tells you how prone it is to mode hops. With spatial hole burning (linear cavity, or ring without an isolator) modes whose x is within half of the excess over threshold also lase; a twisted-mode pair reduces this to 10 %.
  • Filters. Etalons: Airy function with finesse π√R/(1−R), each face of reflectivity R, tilt and tune shift the comb. Lyot filter: plates of thickness d, 2d, 4d in quartz (Δn = 0.0092), T = Π cos²(πτj(ν−νpeak)). Bandpass: Gaussian. Filters are assumed to sit where the beam is collimated enough; a tilted solid etalon in a strongly diverging beam loses finesse, which is not modelled.
  • Linewidth. Single mode: technical linewidth ν·δp/p (path-length noise) added in quadrature to the Schawlow–Townes limit 2πhν(Δνc)²/P. Multimode: the frequency span of the lasing modes.
